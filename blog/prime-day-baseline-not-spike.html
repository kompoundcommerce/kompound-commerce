Prime Day Growth Came From a Bigger Normal Day

Every year sellers judge Prime Day by the size of the spike. I think that's the wrong scoreboard. The brands that grew this year didn't have a bigger event. They walked into the event with a bigger everyday business, and the event simply multiplied it.

The Lesson: The Spike Isn't Where Growth Lives

After a big event, the first question in most brand meetings is "how much did we sell?" The second is usually "how do we make the spike bigger next time?" More deals, more budget, more coupons. I understand the instinct, but I'd push back on it.

An event multiplies whatever business you bring into it. If your normal day is bigger, your event day is bigger, even if the multiplier doesn't change at all. That means the real work happens in the weeks and months before the event, not on event day itself.

This matters because it changes where you put money and attention. If you believe growth comes from the spike, you over-invest in event-day tactics. If you believe growth comes from the base, you invest in the things that lift every day: rank, reviews, conversion, and organic visibility.

How It Works: What the Fall Prime Day Data Showed

The clearest data I've seen on this comes from Mansour Norouzi, Partner and Director of Advertising at Incrementum Digital. On October 13, 2026 he shared results from Fall Prime Day across the US brands Incrementum manages.

Revenue grew, but the base grew more

For brands Incrementum worked with both years, revenue was up 14% year over year. But the base grew 15.5%. Daily sales before the event were already higher than the year before. The event multiplier itself didn't move: event day ran at 1.78x a normal day in both years.

Read that again. The spike was the same size relative to a normal day. All of the year-over-year growth came from a bigger normal day, not a bigger event.

Organic did more of the lifting

The split between organic and paid is the part I find most useful. In Mansour's data, organic sales ran 88% above a normal day during the event, while ad sales ran 51% above, down from 58% the year before. Ads still mattered, but the extra lift came mostly from organic demand.

Not every category spikes

He also noted that bedding, home and apparel spiked hardest, while pet and grocery barely moved. If you sell in a category where shoppers buy on a routine, the event may simply not be your moment, and that's fine.

The takeaway: Prime Day multiplies the business you bring to it. If you want a bigger event, build a bigger normal day first, and judge the event against your own 30-day baseline, not against last year's headline number.

The Summer Event Told the Same Story

Mansour's updates from July Prime Day point the same direction. On June 26, 2026, two days into that event, he reported lift over the prior 30 days of about 1.9x, nearly the same as the year before, with Day 2 growing faster than Day 1.

On June 29, 2026, after 4 days, revenue had lifted 66% over the prior 30 days, compared with 81% the year before. His read: the spike is getting smaller. He was also careful to note that early numbers move as returns and attribution settle, which is a good habit for anyone reporting on an event a day after it ends.

Put the two events side by side and the pattern is hard to ignore. The multiplier isn't growing, and in some cases it's shrinking. The brands that grew did it by raising their baseline.

How I'd Apply It

Measure the event against your own baseline

Stop comparing event day to last year's event day in isolation. Compare it to your own trailing 30-day average. That tells you the true multiplier, and it separates "the event worked" from "the business grew." Those are different questions with different answers.

Watch organic and ad lift separately

If your event lift is mostly paid, you bought it. If it's mostly organic, your listings, rank and reviews earned it. I'd track both, because the organic number tells you how healthy your base really is.

Invest in the everyday base

The highest-return Prime Day prep is the work that lifts every day of the year: better conversion on your main listings, steady review growth, defending rank on the keywords that matter, and keeping inventory in stock so you never lose momentum. A bigger normal day compounds. A bigger one-day spike doesn't.

Set expectations by category

If you sell routine purchases, don't force a spike with deep discounts that train shoppers to wait. Your growth is more likely to come from repeat buying and a steady base than from event day.

Wait before you call the result

Give returns and attribution time to settle before you declare a win or a miss. A number pulled the morning after the event will move.

What to Do This Week

  • Pull your trailing 30-day daily average from before Fall Prime Day and divide event-day sales by it. That's your real multiplier.
  • Do the same for last year's event and compare: did the multiplier grow, or did your base?
  • Split event lift into organic and ad sales, and note which one did more of the work.
  • Check how your category behaved. If it barely moved, plan next year's event budget accordingly.
  • Pick your top listings and list the everyday fixes that would raise their normal-day sales: conversion, reviews, rank and stock.
