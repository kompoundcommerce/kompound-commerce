TikTok Shop Agency Red Flags (and the One Question to Ask First)

Most brands pick a TikTok Shop agency on claimed GMV, a logo wall and a few case studies. I think the real test is what you'd be left with if you ever brought the work in-house. Here are the red flags I'd watch for and the question I'd ask before signing anything.

The Lesson: Hire for the Exit, Not the Pitch

Picking a TikTok Shop agency is one of the highest-leverage calls a brand makes on the platform, and most brands judge it on the wrong things. They look at the GMV an agency claims, the logos on its roster and the case studies in its deck. Those are easy to show and hard to verify, and none of them tell you what you'll be left with when the relationship ends.

My view is simple: the best agency is one that builds a program you could eventually run yourself. The worst is one that builds a program you can never leave. Everything else on this list flows from that.

Before you sign, ask what you'd keep if you brought the work in-house in a year. If the answer is "not much," you're renting a program, not building one.

How It Works: What a Handoff-Ready Agency Looks Like

The clearest example I've seen comes from Joel Contartese, VP of Global Growth at Bloom Nutrition. He shared that Bloom's agency built for handoff from the start. The creator relationships and the tools belonged to Bloom, not the agency. The agency created process documentation along the way. And when the time came, it helped train the people who would replace it.

That's the standard I'd hold any agency to. Joel's advice was to raise this early, with one direct question: "if we brought this in-house in a year, what would we keep?" A good partner answers that without flinching, because it has nothing to hide. An agency whose value depends on holding your creator relationships and your know-how hostage will get vague fast.

Red Flag: "Client" Logos That Are Really TAP Campaigns

This one catches a lot of brands. TAP, the TikTok Affiliate Partner program, lets agencies promote a brand's products through affiliate campaigns. That's a legitimate service. But Joel pointed out that some agencies list every brand they've run TAP campaigns for as a client.

Running affiliate campaigns for a brand is not the same as running its TikTok Shop. Running the Shop means owning sampling, creator strategy, GMV Max, pricing and the P&L. Those are very different jobs, and a logo wall doesn't tell you which one the agency actually did.

The test Joel suggests is the one I'd use: ask to be connected to the person at that brand who runs its TikTok Shop program. Anything other than a clear yes is a red flag.

More Red Flags I'd Walk Away From

Joel also laid out a set of warning signs that match what I'd look for. Here's how I'd read each one.

They push you to commit retainers to high-GMV creators

Big creator names make for impressive pitch decks, but an agency that insists you lock budget into retainers with high-GMV creators is steering you toward the expensive, concentrated version of a creator program. I'd want an agency that earns the right to recommend that spend, not one that leads with it.

TikTok Shop is bolted onto ten other services

If TikTok Shop is one line on a long menu, the agency isn't native to the platform. TikTok Shop moves quickly and rewards specialists. A generalist shop that added it to keep up is usually learning on your budget.

They tell you to focus on one product too early

Narrowing to a single hero SKU sounds disciplined, but on TikTok Shop it can be a mistake early on. Test the catalog first. As Joel put it, your worst DTC SKU can become your number one product on TikTok. You won't find out if the agency picks the winner for you before the platform has had a say.

They're not on Lark

Lark is TikTok's internal communication tool. An agency that isn't on it is a step removed from TikTok's own teams, and that distance shows up when you need answers, support or early information.

How I'd Apply This

Make ownership a contract term, not a hope. Get it in writing that creator relationships, accounts and tools are yours. Ask for process documentation as a deliverable, not a favor.

Verify the roster. For every brand an agency names, ask what it actually ran there: affiliate campaigns, or the full Shop. Then ask for an introduction to the person who owns the program.

Judge the strategy, not the stars. An agency that wants to test your full catalog before betting on a hero product, and that doesn't open with big creator retainers, is thinking about your business rather than its pitch.

Check how close they are to TikTok. Ask directly whether they're on Lark and how they work with TikTok's teams.

What to Do This Week

  • If you're shortlisting agencies, ask each one what you would keep if you brought the program in-house in a year.
  • For every brand on their roster, ask whether they ran the TikTok Shop or only TAP campaigns, and request an intro to the program owner.
  • Ask whether they're on Lark and whether TikTok Shop is their core service.
  • If you already have an agency, confirm in writing who owns your creator relationships, accounts and tools.
  • Pull your catalog and list the SKUs you haven't tested on TikTok Shop yet.
