Your Amazon LTV Is Smaller Than AMC Says

More brands are pulling lifetime value out of Amazon Marketing Cloud and using it to justify a higher acquisition cost. I like that brands are finally looking at repeat behavior. But the number AMC hands you is not the number you should bid against, and the gap between the two is where a lot of ad budgets quietly go wrong.

The Lesson: On Amazon, You Don't Own the Customer

The LTV math most of us learned comes from DTC. You pay once to acquire a customer, then you email them, text them, and sell to them again for close to nothing. If that customer is worth a lot over time, you can afford to pay a lot up front.

Amazon doesn't work that way, and I think the cleanest way to put it is a line from Mansour Norouzi, Partner and Director of Advertising at Incrementum Digital, in a LinkedIn post this August: "In DTC you buy a customer once. On Amazon you rent them."

That's the whole problem in one sentence. Pulling LTV from AMC is a good step. Taking that figure and turning it straight into a target acquisition cost is where it breaks. There are four reasons the real number is smaller than the one on your screen.

How It Works: Four Ways LTV Shrinks

1. Revenue is not money

AMC shows you what a customer spent. That's revenue, not what you keep. Before LTV means anything, subtract COGS, FBA fees, referral fees and returns. Then subtract the one people forget: the Subscribe & Save discount. A lot of the repeat behavior that makes LTV look attractive is driven by that discount, so the repeat revenue that inflates your LTV is also the revenue carrying the thinnest margin.

2. You pay for retention

This is the "rent" part. The customer runs out, searches again, sees your ad, and clicks. You pay for that reorder. And the next one. If you set your acquisition cost from gross LTV, you are budgeting as if those future purchases are free, when in reality you'll pay ad costs on many of them too. You end up paying for the same dollar twice.

3. You don't buy average customers

Your LTV is an average across the customers you already have, and many of them are your loyal core. When you raise your allowable acquisition cost, you don't get more of those people. You win the customers you weren't winning before, the ones who needed broader keywords or deeper discounts to convert. Those customers repeat less than your core. The marginal customer is worth less than the average one.

4. LTV pays over years, the invoice comes this month

Even when the LTV is real, it arrives slowly. Your ad bill doesn't. Cash flow is what actually limits how hard you can push, so the payback period is the real constraint.

The takeaway: Treat LTV as the ceiling on what you could ever pay for a customer, not the target. Net it down to contribution margin, account for the ad cost of every reorder, assume new customers are worse than your average, and let payback period set the pace.

Before You Trust the Number

Even a properly netted LTV is only as good as the data under it. A few things I'd check first.

  • Get enough history. Standard AMC tables stop at around 13 months, which is short for measuring lifetime anything. Mansour pointed to the Amazon Retail Purchases table in AMC, which he said holds up to 5 years of data. He also noted it's a paid feature that is free until the end of 2026, so this is a good time to subscribe.
  • Ignore small cohorts. A handful of customers in a monthly cohort is noise, not a trend. Don't set bids off it.
  • Apply LTV only to new-to-brand customers. Branded clicks were never acquisition. Those shoppers already knew you, so their repeat value shouldn't be used to justify what you pay to find new ones.

How I'd Apply It

I'd build this in two layers, following the order Mansour laid out in a June post on AMC LTV analysis.

Start at the account level

Group customers into monthly cohorts. For each cohort, look at how many customers you acquired, what they spent in month 0, and how many came back in months 1-3. That gives you a grounded view of how quickly customers return and how much of their value shows up early, which is exactly what you need to think about payback.

Then go to the ASIN level

Once the account view is clear, break it down by product. Which ASIN brings new customers in? Which ASINs drive the repeat purchases? Where do customers upgrade to a larger size? This tells you where a higher acquisition cost actually makes sense. An entry ASIN that reliably leads into repeat purchases of other products can justify more spend than one where the first order is also the last.

Set the target from payback, cap it with LTV

With both views in hand, I'd set the acquisition target based on how long you can afford to wait to earn the spend back, and use the netted LTV only as the upper limit. If a target looks fine on LTV but takes longer to pay back than your cash can carry, it's the wrong target.

What to Do This Week

  • Find where your team is using AMC LTV to set acquisition cost, and check whether that figure is gross revenue.
  • Net it down: COGS, FBA, referral fees, returns and the Subscribe & Save discount.
  • Subscribe to the Amazon Retail Purchases table in AMC so you have more than 13 months of history.
  • Filter your LTV analysis to new-to-brand customers and drop cohorts too small to trust.
  • Build the account-level cohort view first, then the ASIN-level view.
  • Rewrite your acquisition targets around payback period, with LTV as the ceiling.
