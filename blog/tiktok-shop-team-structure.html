How to Structure a TikTok Shop Team: Acquisition, Retention, Operations

Running TikTok Shop in-house isn't a one-person job, and it isn't a pile of tasks either. I'd structure the team around three jobs, acquisition, retention and operations, and make sure nothing gets stuck between them. Here's how that works and how I'd set it up.

The Lesson: Build the Team Around the Work, Not the Channel

When a brand decides to run TikTok Shop in-house, the first instinct is to hire "a TikTok person." One generalist who finds creators, ships samples, runs the campaigns, watches inventory and manages GMV Max. That person burns out fast, and the program stalls with them.

I think the better way to structure a TikTok Shop team is around the three jobs the channel actually demands: acquisition, retention and operations. Each one needs a different kind of person, and each one fails in its own predictable way when nobody owns it.

The second half of the lesson matters just as much: those three groups can't work in silos. On TikTok Shop, information has to move constantly between them, or the whole system slows down.

Split the work into acquisition, retention and operations, then make sure information flows freely between them. Retention is the bucket most brands underinvest in, and it's usually why creators leave.

How It Works: The Three Buckets

Joel Contartese, VP of Global Growth at Bloom Nutrition, laid out this exact structure, and it's the clearest version I've seen. Here's how I'd describe each bucket.

Acquisition: keep the top of the funnel full

This group grows the creator pipeline. Their job is to bring new creators in, constantly, so you never depend on the same small group. Outreach, recruiting and first contact all sit here. If acquisition goes quiet, you won't feel it today. You'll feel it a month from now, when your active creator count quietly shrinks.

Retention: the most underrated bucket

Retention owns the creator experience and the relationships. In Joel's framing, this is the most underrated part of the team, and it's usually the reason creators leave. The roles here need people who understand what creators need and then actually deliver it: fast answers, samples that arrive, clear briefs, and someone who notices when a good creator goes silent.

I'd go further: most brands staff acquisition heavily and leave retention to whoever has spare time. That's backwards. Every creator you lose to a bad experience is one your acquisition team has to replace.

Operations: everything that makes the shop run

Operations covers go-to-market, campaigns, forecasting, inventory planning, store management and GMV Max. It's the least glamorous bucket and the one that decides whether your creators' content actually converts into orders you can fulfill.

Why No Silos Matters

The structure only works if the buckets talk to each other all the time. Joel gave two examples that show why. A creator's comment on a call can shape a campaign. A campaign can change forecasting. Retention hears something, operations plans around it, and acquisition recruits for it.

If your retention lead hears that creators are struggling with a product angle and that never reaches the person planning next month's campaign, you've wasted the most valuable signal you have. If operations plans a big push without telling the team that recruits and supports creators, you'll have inventory and no content, or content and no inventory.

Proof That It Holds Up Under Pressure

Bloom is a useful case because it made the switch recently. Joel shared that Bloom went fully in-house on TikTok Shop after working with agencies. Its first in-house month went straight into DFYD, a major TikTok Shop campaign, plus Bloom's first Super Brand Day.

According to Joel, the team finished #2 in GMV goal attainment in Health and top 5 overall, while absorbing a 7-figure-per-month shop with thousands of affiliates. He also credited the previous agency for a smooth transition, which is worth noting: going in-house doesn't have to mean a messy handoff.

That's the point I'd take from it. A clear structure is what lets a team take on a large shop and a major campaign at the same time without dropping balls.

The Foundation Underneath the Team

Structure isn't enough on its own. Joel also described the basics every TikTok Shop program needs, and I'd treat these as the operating checklist each bucket supports:

  • Creator outreach, using tools like Cruva (acquisition).
  • Inventory to sell and to sample, with fast restocks (operations).
  • A Discord or Lark for creators, so the community has a home (retention).
  • Content volume, which all three buckets feed.
  • A healthy GMV Max budget with realistic ROAS (operations).

On inventory, his warning is one I'd repeat to any brand: a few days out of stock can create FOMO, but weeks out of stock kills momentum. That's why forecasting and inventory planning belong inside the TikTok Shop team, not in a separate department that hears about campaigns late.

How I'd Apply This

Name an owner for each bucket. Even on a small team where one person covers two buckets, write down who owns acquisition, retention and operations. Unowned work is the work that slips.

Staff retention on purpose. Hire or assign someone whose job is creator experience, not someone who handles it between other tasks. Look for people who understand creators and follow through.

Build the information loops. Set up a short recurring sync where retention shares what creators are saying, operations shares upcoming campaigns and stock levels, and acquisition shares who's coming in. Keep it practical.

Pull inventory planning closer. Make sure whoever forecasts stock for TikTok Shop sees campaign plans early and hears from the creator side directly.

If you're leaving an agency, plan the handoff. Map each thing the agency did today to one of the three buckets before you take it over.

What to Do This Week

  • List every recurring TikTok Shop task and sort it into acquisition, retention or operations.
  • Put a single owner's name next to each bucket.
  • Check whether anyone is responsible for creator experience full-time. If not, decide who will be.
  • Set up a recurring cross-team sync and a shared channel for creator feedback.
  • Review stock for your top TikTok Shop products against upcoming campaigns and sampling needs.
