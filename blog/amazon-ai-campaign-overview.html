Amazon's AI Campaign Recommendations: Good Analysis, Missing Context

Amazon now puts an AI summary at the top of your Sponsored Products campaigns, complete with one-click fixes. It's tempting to hit accept and move on. My advice: don't, at least not until you've checked two things the tool can't check for you.

The Lesson: A Recommendation Is Only as Good as the Goal Behind It

Automated suggestions in ad consoles aren't new, but this one is more prominent and more persuasive than most. It sits right at the top of the campaign, it explains its reasoning in plain language, and it lets you apply changes with a single click. That combination makes it very easy to accept advice without asking whether it fits the campaign in front of you.

My view is that these tools are useful for spotting things you might have missed, and risky as decision-makers. They read the numbers. They don't know why the campaign exists. And on Amazon, the reason a campaign exists changes what "good performance" means.

If you manage ads with any kind of strategy beyond pure efficiency, you need to treat every one-click fix as a question, not an answer.

How It Works: A Real Example

The clearest breakdown I've seen of the new feature comes from Mansour Norouzi, Partner and Director of Advertising at Incrementum Digital. On July 31, 2026 he shared what showed up in one of his Sponsored Products campaigns: a new box labeled "Campaign overview (Beta)," with an AI-written summary and one-click fixes.

The suggestions in his example looked reasonable on the surface:

  • Add 10 negative exact keywords. Together they had spent $117.50 with 0 purchases.
  • Cut the top keyword's bid from $11.34 to $7.94.
  • Pause two targets that had spent $78.46 with no sales.

Any of those could be the right call in some account. But when Mansour looked at the data behind them, two problems showed up.

Problem one: the sample sizes were tiny

Nine of the ten search terms the tool wanted to negate had exactly one click each. That's not evidence of a losing term. A search term with one click and no sale hasn't failed; it simply hasn't had a chance yet. Negating it locks you out of finding out whether it would have converted with more traffic.

This is a mistake I see people make manually too, so I'm not surprised software makes it. Spend with no purchases looks bad in a summary line. Spread across a handful of single-click terms, it's mostly noise.

Problem two: it doesn't know what the campaign is for

The bigger flaw, as Mansour pointed out, is that the tool assumes every campaign is trying to be efficient. Plenty aren't. In a ranking campaign, a high ACoS is the plan. You're deliberately paying more to win sales and placement on a keyword you want to rank for. Cutting the bid on the top keyword by roughly 30% would work directly against that goal.

His summary of the feature was short and fair:

Mansour Norouzi

"The analysis is decent. The context is missing."

The takeaway: Before you accept any AI recommendation, check the data behind it and know the campaign's goal. The tool can read your numbers; only you know what the campaign is supposed to do.

How I'd Apply It

Label every campaign with its job

If a tool can't see a campaign's goal, make sure your team can. I'd name or tag campaigns by purpose: ranking, defense, profit, discovery. When a recommendation appears, the first question is whether it serves that purpose. An efficiency fix on a profit campaign may be exactly right. The same fix on a ranking campaign can undo weeks of work.

Open the data before you click

A summary line like "spent with no purchases" hides how that spend is distributed. Click into the search terms or targets the tool is flagging and look at clicks per term. If most of them have barely any traffic, the right move is usually to wait, not to negate.

Use it as a second pair of eyes

I wouldn't ignore the feature. It's a quick way to surface targets that are drifting, and occasionally it will catch something real. Treat it like a junior analyst's notes: worth reading, worth checking, not worth applying without review.

Be careful with bid cuts on your top keyword

Your highest-spending keyword is often your most strategic one. Big bid changes there deserve the most scrutiny, because they ripple into placement, sales velocity and, over time, organic rank.

What to Do This Week

  • Open the Campaign overview box on your largest Sponsored Products campaigns and read what it's suggesting.
  • For any negative keyword suggestion, check the click count on each search term before accepting.
  • Write down the goal of each active campaign: ranking, profit, defense or discovery.
  • Reject or hold any bid cut that conflicts with a campaign's goal, especially on top keywords.
  • Make sure anyone with access to the account knows not to apply one-click fixes without review.
