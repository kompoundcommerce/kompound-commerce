Subscribe & Save vs. Reorder Customers: Same LTV, Different Economics

When your repeat buyers and your subscribers show similar lifetime value, it's tempting to conclude that Subscribe & Save doesn't matter. I think that's the wrong read. LTV tells you what a customer is worth. It doesn't tell you what it costs to get their next order.

The Lesson: Look at the Cost of the Next Order, Not Just LTV

Most Amazon brands judge their customers by lifetime value. That's a reasonable starting point, but it hides something important. Two customers can end up worth roughly the same amount and still cost you very different amounts to keep buying.

A customer who reorders on their own still has to come back through the front door. They search again. They see your ad and a competitor's ad on the same page. They may need a retargeting touch to remember you, or a coupon to choose you over the cheaper option. Every one of those steps is a place where you pay, and a place where you can lose them.

A subscriber skips all of that. Their next order is already on the calendar. You aren't bidding to win it back, and you aren't discounting to rescue it.

Equal LTV doesn't mean equal profit. Ask how much it costs to generate the next order from each customer type. A subscriber's next order is already scheduled. A reorder customer's next order has to be won again.

How It Works: What the New Subscribe & Save Data Shows

This question got much easier to answer this fall. Mansour Norouzi, Partner and Director of Advertising at Incrementum Digital, flagged on Sep 3, 2026 that Amazon's Subscribe & Save dashboard had added new graphs:

  • S&S sales by number of deliveries, broken out by 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5+ deliveries.
  • Coupon-driven units, split by S&S coupon, reorder coupon and standard coupon.
  • LTV by segment: one-time, reorder and subscriber customers.
  • Customer share by segment, so you can see how your base is actually distributed.

The delivery counts are the piece I'd look at first. Sign-ups only tell you how many people opted in. Deliveries tell you how many stayed. That's the real retention picture, and until now most brands were guessing at it.

The coupon split matters too. When S&S coupons, reorder coupons and standard coupons are lumped together, you can't tell whether you're paying to acquire customers or paying to keep them. Separating them starts to show which incentives do which job.

Then came the interesting part. In Mansour's own data, reorder customer LTV came out close to subscriber LTV. He asked the obvious question, whether subscribing matters at all, and his answer was yes, for exactly the reason above: the reorder customer may have to search again, click an ad, get retargeted or need a coupon, while the subscriber's next order is already set.

He also shared a related finding from Amazon Marketing Cloud on Oct 19: customers reordered later than assumed, and coffee buyers who started on a 2-pack or bigger repeated about twice as often. That's a reminder that your assumptions about reorder timing deserve a check against real data.

How I'd Apply This

Price the next order, not just the customer. LTV is revenue. What you want to know is margin after the cost of getting each order. For reorder customers, that means the ad clicks, retargeting and coupons it took to bring them back. If you can't measure it precisely, even a rough estimate will show you the gap.

Treat satisfied reorder customers as your best S&S prospects. They've already bought more than once, so the product fit is proven. The opportunity is to move them toward Subscribe & Save so their next order stops costing you ad spend. This is a much easier conversion than convincing a first-time buyer to subscribe.

Use delivery counts as your retention metric. Stop celebrating sign-ups on their own. Watch how many subscribers make it past their first and second deliveries. If people drop off early, that's a product, pricing or cadence problem worth fixing before you push more sign-ups.

Separate acquisition coupons from retention coupons. Look at the coupon split and ask what each type is doing. If most of your discounting goes to bringing reorder customers back, that's spend a subscription could replace.

Check your reorder timing. If your customers reorder later than you think, your retargeting and email timing is probably too early, and your subscription default cadence may be off too. And if starting on a larger pack makes repeat buying more likely in your category, it's worth testing which entry size you lead with.

Why This Matters More Than It Looks

Subscribe & Save often gets treated as a checkbox: turn it on, set a discount, move on. But the economics are different from regular repeat purchases. A reorder customer keeps competing for space in the search results every cycle, and you keep paying to win them. A subscriber has taken themselves out of that auction.

That doesn't mean you should chase subscribers at any cost. It means that when the LTV numbers look equal, you shouldn't stop there. The cheaper customer to keep is usually the more profitable one, and that's where I'd focus the effort.

What to Do This Week

  • Open your Subscribe & Save dashboard and review sales by number of deliveries to see where subscribers drop off.
  • Compare LTV and customer share for your one-time, reorder and subscriber segments.
  • Review the coupon split and note how much of your discounting goes to S&S, reorder and standard coupons.
  • Estimate what it costs in ads, retargeting and coupons to generate the next order from a reorder customer.
  • Pick one way to nudge satisfied reorder customers toward Subscribe & Save, and test it.
