The Real TikTok Shop Problem Is Keeping Creators, Not Finding Them

Most brands treat their TikTok Shop affiliate program as a recruiting problem. Get more creators, send more samples, raise the commission. I think that framing is backwards. The bigger leak is the creators who join, post a few videos, and quietly disappear.

The Lesson: Recruiting Is Easy, Retention Is the Work

Getting creators to sign up for your affiliate program is not hard. Getting them to stick around long enough to actually sell is. That's the part most brands underinvest in, and it's where I'd put attention before spending another dollar on outreach.

Here's why it matters. Creators are being pitched constantly. Free samples, special offers, higher commission rates. Every brand in your category is offering some version of the same deal. If a creator doesn't start earning with you quickly, there's always another brand waiting with a better rate, and they move on.

The math makes that worse. It can take hundreds of videos before a creator lands a meaningful sale on a product. If your program loses people after a handful of posts, you're churning creators right before the point where they would have started paying off. You paid the cost of recruiting them and never collected the return.

How It Works: The Leak Is in the Relationship

One of the clearest examples of this comes from Joel Contartese, VP of Global Growth at Bloom Nutrition. Writing about his costliest mistakes, he said he assumed the problem was finding creators. In reality, his team was great at recruiting and bad at keeping them. The leak was retention, relationships and the overall creator experience. Earlier in the year he made the same point from the other direction: getting creators in was never Bloom's problem.

What fills that gap? According to Joel, what most creators don't get from brands is access. A real community. A place to learn from each other, hear what's working, and feel like they're part of something rather than one name on a commission list.

He tested that idea in person. Joel and a colleague hosted more than 10 creators at Bloom HQ on a Sunday. They planned for one hour and ended up spending three. There was a tour, a Q&A, and time spent asking the creators directly how Bloom could be better. His conclusion was that everyone offers commission, and going beyond the transaction is what separates a brand.

Later, at Social Commerce Fest in LA, he talked with hundreds of creators and came away with a consistent picture of what the best ones want:

  • They turn down money for products they don't believe in.
  • Full-time creating is isolating, so community matters to them.
  • They want to be trusted, not scripted.
  • They want to join events and meet the people leading the brand.

The detail that stuck with me most: most of what creators asked for didn't mention money at all.

The takeaway: Commission gets a creator in the door, but every brand offers commission. What keeps the best creators posting long enough to sell is community, trust and access to your team. Build that before you scale recruitment.

How I'd Apply It

If I were running a TikTok Shop affiliate program today, I'd put a retention plan in place before I scaled recruiting. Adding more creators to a program that can't hold onto them just makes the leak bigger. Here's what that plan would include.

A regular touchpoint

Set up a weekly call that any active creator can join. Share what's working: hooks, formats, angles that are converting. Let creators share with each other too. This is the "place to learn" that most programs never offer, and it costs you very little to run.

A real feedback loop

Ask creators how you could be better, and mean it. What's confusing about the product? What do their viewers ask about? What would make it easier to post? Creators are closer to your customer than most of your team is. Treat their input as product and marketing research, not a courtesy.

Recognition that isn't only a payout

Call out creators who are putting in the work, not just the ones who already hit big numbers. Remember that early on, a creator may be posting for a long time before the sales show up. Recognition keeps them going through that stretch, when it's easiest to quit.

Events and access to leadership

You don't need a big budget to do what Bloom did. An in-person meetup, a virtual session with your founder, or a behind-the-scenes look at how the product gets made all signal that a creator is part of something. Creators want to meet the people behind the brand. Let them.

Trust over scripts

Give creators the key facts and the claims they need to get right, then let them make the content their own way. The best creators turn down products they don't believe in. Over-scripting tells them you don't trust them, and it usually makes the content worse.

What to Do This Week

  • Look at your affiliate roster and identify creators who posted a few times and then stopped.
  • Reach out to a handful of them and ask what would have kept them posting.
  • Schedule a recurring weekly call for active creators and share what's converting.
  • Pick one way to recognize creators that isn't a commission bump.
  • Plan a first event, in person or virtual, where creators can meet your team.
  • Hold off on scaling outreach until the retention pieces are running.
