Amazon Sponsored Prompts: What 165 Prompts Across 6 Brands Show

Sponsored Prompts aren't free anymore, so they deserve the same scrutiny as any other ad dollar. The early data says the prompt type matters far more than the placement, and the attribution story is still murky. Here's how I'd read the numbers and how I'd manage prompts from here.

The Lesson: Treat Prompts Like a Real Channel Now

Sponsored Prompts, the ads that show up as suggested questions in Amazon's AI shopping experience, started out free in beta. That changed. Once something costs money, "it's free, leave it on" stops being a strategy, and you need an actual point of view on what's working.

My take: most of the value in Sponsored Prompts comes from answering the questions shoppers already have about the product, not from pushing the brand. And before anyone scales spend, they should be honest about how little we know yet about what prompts actually cause versus what they simply get credit for.

Prompts that answer real product questions earn the clicks. Prompts that sell the brand mostly don't. Review them every week, and don't scale until you've decided how you'll judge their incremental value.

How It Works: What the Data Shows

The best dataset I've seen on this comes from Mansour Norouzi, Partner and Director of Advertising at Incrementum Digital. On April 30, 2026, right as prompts moved out of free beta, he shared aggregated data across 6 brands: 165 unique prompts and roughly 540 placements.

CPCs are all over the place

In his data, CPCs ranged from $0.02 to $5.40. That spread tells you two things. First, pricing on this placement isn't settled, so cheap clicks exist if you're paying attention. Second, a prompt that's quietly costing you top-of-range CPCs can drain budget without anyone noticing, because nobody's looking at prompts line by line.

Prompt type decides click-through

This is the part I'd pay the most attention to. Mansour broke CTR out by the kind of prompt:

  • Product questions (the "does it do X?" style): 4.11% CTR.
  • Feature or benefit prompts (a feature callout with an invitation to learn more): 3.93% CTR.
  • Brand-specific questions (asking whether a brand makes a certain product): 0.41% CTR.
  • Brand awareness prompts (asking why someone should choose the brand): 0.21% CTR.

The pattern is hard to miss. Shoppers click when a prompt matches a question they're already trying to answer about the product in front of them. They mostly ignore prompts that read like the brand talking about itself. That lines up with how people use an AI shopping assistant: they're there to get answers, not to hear a pitch.

The Two Questions I'd Ask Before Scaling

Mansour was upfront that the data raises questions it can't answer yet. I think both are the right ones to ask, and I'd want answers before putting serious budget behind prompts.

Open question 1: Do prompts drive the purchase, or skim credit?

A shopper who sees your prompt has often already found you through Sponsored Products or organic rank. So how much of the sale is the prompt actually driving, and how much is it taking credit for work your SP campaigns and organic efforts already did? If you can't separate those, a strong-looking prompt report may just be relabeling sales you'd have gotten anyway.

Open question 2: Who gets the credit?

Attribution between prompts and campaigns is the second problem. Mansour suspects prompt reports show prompt-attributed sales while campaign-level reporting still credits the last ad click. If that's right, the same sale can look like a win in two places. It gets messier when prompts show up as a step in the path in AMC. Until that's clearer, I wouldn't add prompt sales and campaign sales together and call it total ad impact.

The Underrated Upside: Free Customer Research

Here's the part I like most. Even if the attribution never gets clean, prompt reports are great customer research. They show you what shoppers ask about your product, in their own framing. That's input for your listing copy, your images, your A+ content and your FAQ, and it's a good check on whether your listing answers the questions people actually have.

Spend share on prompts is still tiny, but it's growing. Incrementum responded by adding a prompt review to its weekly process, where each prompt gets one of three calls: keep, pause or test. I think that's exactly the right level of attention for a channel this size. Not a big strategy overhaul, just a standing habit.

How I'd Apply This

Lead with product questions and feature prompts. Those are the formats that earned clicks in the data. Write prompts around the questions a hesitant shopper would actually ask about fit, function or use.

Be skeptical of brand-led prompts. Brand-specific and brand awareness prompts had the weakest CTRs by a wide margin. If you run them, give them a clear reason to exist and watch them closely.

Watch CPC prompt by prompt. With a range that wide, don't manage prompts as one blended line. Find the expensive ones and decide whether they've earned it.

Don't double count. Keep prompt-attributed sales and campaign-attributed sales in separate columns until you understand how they overlap.

Mine the reports. Every few weeks, pull the questions shoppers are asking and feed them back into your listing and content work.

What to Do This Week

  • Pull your Sponsored Prompts report and tag each prompt by type: product question, feature/benefit, brand-specific or brand awareness.
  • Compare CTR and CPC by type, and flag the most expensive prompts.
  • Give every prompt a call: keep, pause or test.
  • List the top shopper questions from the report and check whether your listing answers each one.
  • Add a short prompt review to your weekly ads meeting.
