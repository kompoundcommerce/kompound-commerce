When AI Is Confidently Wrong About Your Amazon Data

The most dangerous AI answer isn't the one that's obviously broken. It's the clean, confident, well-written one that's wrong. Here's why that happens with Amazon data, and how I'd set AI up so it stops happening to you.

The Lesson: Polish Is Not Proof

AI has become part of how sellers analyze their accounts. You paste in a report, ask what happened, and get back a tidy explanation in seconds. That speed is real, and I'd encourage every seller to use it. But there's a trap built into it: AI writes a wrong answer with exactly the same confidence and polish as a right one.

When an answer is obviously off, you catch it. When it reads well, sounds sure of itself and fits the numbers on the surface, you move on. That's the failure mode I'd worry about most. AI is incredible when you know enough to catch where it slips, and risky in exactly the places where you can't.

The takeaway: AI doesn't know what it doesn't know about Amazon. Give it the platform context it's missing, and check its reasoning in the areas where you're the expert.

How It Works: The Attribution Trap

The best example I've seen of this comes from Mansour Norouzi, Partner and Director of Advertising at Incrementum Digital. On Jun 11, 2026, he shared that while analyzing Prime Day data, AI gave him a clean, confident, well-written explanation. He almost moved on. The whole reasoning was wrong.

Why the AI got it wrong

The cause was a detail of how Amazon reports ad sales. When a shopper clicks an ad and buys days later, the sale is credited to the date of the click, not the date of the purchase. The AI didn't know that. So it read every sale as if it had happened on the day it showed up in the report, and built a full story on top of that misreading.

Think about what that does to a Prime Day analysis, where timing is everything. If your tool assumes every sale happened on the day it's credited, its explanation of which day worked and why can be confidently, completely wrong.

Why it matters more than an obvious error

Once Mansour explained attribution to it, the AI caught the mistake instantly and became useful again. The tool wasn't the problem. The missing context was. His summary of the risk is the line I'd tape to every analyst's monitor: "Confident plus wrong is harder to catch than obviously wrong." He also pointed out that a beginner would have published it. He still uses AI daily; he just checks the details in his own field.

What the right reading looks like

The flip side is what happens when the context is in place. In a separate post on Jun 29, Mansour described how his team read Day 4 of Prime Day 2025. It was strong, but not because of new traffic. It was strong because shoppers who had carted products earlier came back and checked out. Knowing that, they held Day 4 spend flat in 2026 instead of chasing it, and Day 4 still finished second-best. That's the kind of decision a context-blind analysis would get backwards.

How I'd Apply It

I don't think the answer is to trust AI less across the board. It's to stop expecting it to know your platform the way you do, and to build that knowledge into how you use it.

Feed it your knowledge, not just your data

A general AI model, left to its own devices, gives you roughly what the internet as a whole believes about your question: the average answer. Average is rarely what you want for your account. Mansour addressed this in an Apr 15, 2026 post: he built a personal wiki of everything he knows, from course material to talks to team sessions, and now client analyses run against his own frameworks and diagnostics. I'd recommend every serious seller or agency do some version of this. Write down how Amazon actually behaves, the rules you've learned, and the diagnostics you trust, and make that part of every prompt.

Start with the mechanics that fool it most

Attribution windows are the obvious first entry. Any explanation of how Amazon credits ad sales, and when they appear in reports, belongs in the context you give AI before it touches your ad data. From there, add whatever platform rules have tripped up your own analysis in the past.

Check hardest where it sounds surest

When an AI answer is especially neat, treat that as a cue to verify, not to relax. Ask it to show which assumption each conclusion rests on. If one of those assumptions is about how Amazon reports something, confirm it yourself before you act. And if you're new to Amazon advertising, pair AI output with someone who knows the platform until you can spot these slips on your own.

What to Do This Week

  • Pick one recent AI-generated analysis of your ad data and check whether it accounted for sales being credited to the click date.
  • Start a simple document of Amazon rules and diagnostics you rely on, beginning with attribution, and paste it into your AI sessions.
  • Before your next event, look at whether strong days came from new traffic or from shoppers who carted earlier, and plan spend accordingly.
  • Add one step to your review process: ask AI to list its assumptions, then verify the Amazon-specific ones.
